LADDER LINE, DOUBLETS & THE G5RV

One wire, one feedline, every band. How doublets fed with ladder line work, the popular lengths (88 ft, 44 ft, G5RV, ZS6BKW), how to pick a feeder length your tuner will like, and why the "lossy" feedline usually isn't.

THE BIG IDEA

Why Ladder Line?

A doublet is a dipole you don't cut for one band. On most bands the feedpoint is a bad match, sometimes very bad. With coax, that mismatch burns power in the cable. With ladder line it barely costs anything, and the tuner at the radio takes care of the rest.

LOW LOSS

SWR Doesn't Hurt It

Ladder line (450 Ω "window line" or 300 Ω twin-lead) has very little loss to begin with. Even at an SWR of 10:1 on the line, 100 ft of window line usually loses less than 1 dB. The same SWR on RG-58 can lose most of your power. Try the numbers

ONE ANTENNA

Every Band

An 88 ft doublet with ladder line and a decent tuner works 80 through 10 m. A 44 ft doublet covers 40–10 m. No traps, no links, no band changes in the field.

THE CATCH

You Need a Tuner

Ladder line is balanced, so it needs a balanced tuner or a balun at the tuner. It also has to be kept away from metal. And some feeder lengths put an impedance at the tuner that it can't match. The checker helps you avoid those.

PICK ONE

Popular Designs

All are center-fed wires. The difference is the length and how they're fed.

HOW THEY LOOK

Doublet vs. G5RV

Doublet: ladder line all the way to the tuner. This is the most flexible and the lowest loss. G5RV / ZS6BKW: a cut-to-length piece of ladder line, then coax. It's easier to bring indoors, but there's more loss on the bands it doesn't match.

THE LENGTHS

Cheat Sheet

88 FT DOUBLET2 × 44 ft legs. Ladder line to a tuner. 80–10 m (60 m too). The classic all-band wire.
44 FT DOUBLET2 × 22 ft legs. 40–10 m, and 80 m with a good tuner (it's short there). Fits most campsites and a 31 ft pole as an inverted V.
135 FT DOUBLET2 × 67.5 ft. 160 m with a tuner, strong on 80/40. Needs room.
G5RV102 ft top + 34 ft open-wire section (about 30.5 ft of 450 Ω window line, or 28.5 ft of 300 Ω twin-lead), then coax. Low SWR on 20 m. A tuner is needed on most other bands.
G5RV JR51 ft top + 17 ft open wire (about 15.3 ft of window line). 40–10 m with a tuner.
ZS6BKW93.5 ft top + about 40–42 ft of 450 Ω window line, then coax. Often under 2:1 on 40, 20, 17, 12 and 10 m without a tuner. Trim the matching section for your height.

Use the checker below to try your own lengths. Inverted-V or flat, these all work. The ends just need to stay out of reach.

CALCULATOR

Feeder Length Checker

Some combinations of leg length and ladder line length put a very high impedance at your tuner on a band. That makes it hard to tune, and it's hard on the tuner. Pick your antenna and feeder, see which bands look good, and get a better feeder length if one band is bad.

AT THE RADIO

Tuner & Balun

Ladder line is balanced. Most tuners and every radio are unbalanced (coax). Something has to bridge the two.

BEST

Balanced Tuner

A true balanced tuner (link-coupled, or a balanced L-network) takes ladder line straight in. It has the widest range and the best balance. They cost more, but nothing beats one for a doublet.

MOST COMMON

1:1 Current Balun + Tuner

Put a 1:1 current balun right at the tuner's output (or outside the window), then a short coax jumper to the tuner. 1:1 handles the widest range of impedances. A 4:1 balun helps when the impedance is high on most bands, but it can make the low-impedance bands harder.

PORTABLE RIGS

Built-In Tuners

Internal tuners in portable rigs typically only match up to about 10:1. Pair them with a 4:1 balun and pick the feeder length with the checker. Or carry a small external manual tuner. That's the real portable doublet secret.

DO'S & DON'TS

Handling Ladder Line

  • Keep it away from metal: gutters, towers, masts, siding. Give it 6" or more (a few times its width). Metal nearby unbalances it and it starts to radiate.
  • Don't tape it to the mast. Use standoffs, or hang it free. A bit of slack and swing is fine.
  • A twist every foot or two stops it from flapping in the wind and fatiguing the wire.
  • Come off the antenna at a right angle for as far as you can before turning.
  • Entering the house: a window feedthrough panel, or switch to coax through a 1:1 balun outside the window. A few feet of coax after the balun is fine.
  • Water: wet window line shifts tuning a bit. Wet flat TV twin-lead gets lossy. Use window line outdoors.
  • Strain relief at the center insulator. The copper-clad steel in window line breaks if it flexes at one spot. Use a center insulator made for ladder line.
  • Lightning: disconnect and ground the line when you're not using it. Ladder line can't go through a regular coax arrestor.
TAKE IT TO THE PARK

The Portable Doublet

A 44 ft doublet on a fiberglass pole is one of the best all-band portable antennas there is. No band changes, no links.

THE BUILD

Parts

  • Two 22 ft legs of light wire (26 AWG silicone or 20 AWG stranded)
  • A small center insulator, or a 3D-printed or plastic block
  • 25–35 ft of lightweight 300 Ω twin-lead or thin 450 Ω line (a lighter, cheaper option: twisted speaker wire, at some loss)
  • A small 4:1 or 1:1 balun with a BNC, a short coax jumper, and a manual tuner or your rig's tuner
  • A 20–31 ft fiberglass pole. Set it up as an inverted V with the ends 6–8 ft up
SETUP

In the Field

  1. Pole up, center at the top, legs sloping out about 90–120° apart.
  2. Feeder straight down the pole (a couple of inches off it with a clip) to the balun at the bottom.
  3. Tune each band once and write down the tuner settings for next time.
  4. A band won't tune? Add or remove 5–8 ft of feeder (coil the extra in a loose figure-8, not tight on the ground). Or run the checker before you go.
