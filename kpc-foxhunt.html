FOX HUNTING

Somebody hides a transmitter. Everybody else finds it with a radio, a directional antenna and a little detective work. Here's how to hunt, how to build a $20 beam, and a map that plots your bearings for you.

WHAT IT ISHOW TO HUNTBEARING MAPBUILD A BEAMCLOSE-IN GEARHIDE A FOXARDF
THE BASICS

Three Ways To Hunt a Fox

A "fox" is a small, low-power transmitter — usually on 2 meters FM — that sends a tone, a short message or CW and its callsign. Your local hunt sets the frequency; many U.S. hunts use 146.565 MHz simplex.

BY CAR

Mobile T-Hunt

Teams drive, take bearings from high open spots, plot them on a map and close in. Usually the fox is hidden somewhere within a few miles. Lowest time or mileage wins.

  • Beam out the window or on a mast
  • Navigator plots, driver drives
  • Watch for signal reflections off hills
ON FOOT

Foxoring & Park Hunts

One or more foxes hidden in a park or woods — perfect for a KPC campout. Hunters walk with an HT and a handheld beam. Great for families and new hams.

  • An HT + tape-measure Yagi is all you need
  • Short distances, lots of fun close-in
  • No license needed to listen and hunt
THE SPORT

ARDF

Amateur Radio Direction Finding — the international, orienteering-style version. Five foxes in the woods, one minute each, a map and a compass. There are U.S. and world championships.

  • 2 m and 80 m events
  • Age and gender categories
  • No GPS allowed
STEP BY STEP

How To Find the Fox

  1. Get high and in the openTake your first bearings from a hilltop or open field, away from buildings and metal. Reflections fool you most in valleys and parking lots.
  2. Swing the beam, find the nullRotate slowly. The strongest signal is out the front — but the sharp drop off the back of a Yagi is often easier to pinpoint. Point the back at the fox, then turn around.
  3. Write it downBearing in degrees, where you were, and the time. Or tap Take bearing on the map below — it records your GPS spot and compass heading for you.
  4. Move sideways, take anotherA second bearing from a spot a good distance to the side gives you a crossing point. A third one shows how good your fix is — the smaller the triangle, the better.
  5. Close in, cut the signalAs it gets strong, everything sounds like "everywhere." Switch to an attenuator, the offset attenuator, the 3rd harmonic trick or body fade (below).
  6. The last 100 feetTake the antenna off your HT, hold the radio against your body and turn in a circle — your body blocks the signal. Look up, look down: foxes hide in trees, ammo cans, under picnic tables.

Magnetic vs. true north: a compass (and your phone) points to magnetic north. Maps use true north. In Kentucky, magnetic north is a few degrees west of true — the bearing map corrects this for you.

WEEKEND PROJECT

Build a Tape-Measure Yagi

The classic fox-hunting beam: three elements cut from a steel tape measure on a PVC boom. It folds flat, costs about $20, and has a deep null off the back. Dimensions below are the popular WB2HOL design for 2 m — change the frequency to scale it.

PARTCUT TO
  1. Cut the tape. Use a 1" wide steel tape. Cut with tin snips (it's spring steel — gloves and safety glasses). Cover every cut end with electrical tape.
  2. Driven element. Two equal halves, about 1" apart in the middle. Sand the paint off where the coax solders on.
  3. Hairpin match. Bend the wire into a U (legs about ¾" apart) and solder its ends across the two driven halves.
  4. Coax. About 3 ft of thin coax: center to one driven half, shield to the other. Tape it to the boom.
  5. Mount. Hose clamps hold each element to a PVC cross (driven) or tee (reflector, director). Don't glue — it comes apart for the car.
  6. Tune. Check SWR at your frequency and slide the driven halves closer or farther apart until it's lowest.

THE LAST STRETCH

Close-In Gear & Tricks

Near the fox, the signal floods your radio from every direction and the beam stops helping. These cut it back down so you can keep homing in.

BEST TOOL

Offset Attenuator

A tiny box between the beam and your HT. It mixes the fox signal with a 4 MHz oscillator, so you listen about 4 MHz away (146.565 fox → 150.565 or 142.565) and turn a knob to make the signal as weak as you need — even right next to the fox.

Your HT must receive outside the ham band. Homebrew kits are common club builds.

FREE TRICK

Listen on the 3rd Harmonic

Every transmitter leaks a weak copy at 3× its frequency. A 146.565 fox also shows up faintly on 439.695 MHz. Tune your dual-band HT there when the main signal is pinning the meter.

Works only when you're quite close.

NO GEAR

Body Fade

Take the antenna off (or turn the squelch off and use a rubber duck), hold the HT flat against your chest and turn slowly in a circle. The signal dips when your body is between the radio and the fox — the fox is behind you.

Also try: step attenuator, tuning slightly off frequency, and pointing the beam up and down.

FOR THE HOST

Hiding a Fox

Running a hunt at a campout or club meeting? A few things make it fair, fun and legal.

THE TRANSMITTER

What to Use

  • An old HT with a "fox controller," or a ready-made fox box
  • Low power — under a watt is plenty for a park hunt
  • Transmit every 30–60 seconds so hunters get bearings
  • Plenty of battery for the whole hunt (plus extra)
THE RULES

Keep It Legal

  • The fox must send its callsign at least every 10 minutes (CW ID is fine)
  • Use a simplex frequency your area uses for hunts — not a repeater input or the 146.52 calling frequency
  • Get permission to hide it on park or private property
MAKE IT FUN

Good Hiding Spots

  • Start easy for new hunters — in sight from a trail
  • Ammo cans, tree branches, under benches
  • Hide near metal for a challenge (reflections!)
  • Leave a logbook or prize inside for finders
THE INTERNATIONAL SPORT

ARDF in a Nutshell

Amateur Radio Direction Finding blends fox hunting with orienteering. It's run under IARU rules worldwide.

Bands2 m (FM/AM tones) and 80 m (CW)
FoxesFive low-power foxes on one frequency, each on for one minute in turn
IDsEach sends Morse "MO" plus its number: MOE, MOI, MOS, MOH, MO5
FinishA beacon at the finish sends all the time on its own frequency
CourseAbout 6–12 km in the woods on an orienteering map, 2–3 hour limit
RulesOn foot, no GPS. Categories by age and gender set how many foxes you need

Shorter versions — sprint and foxoring (low-power foxes at marked points on the map) — are great starter events and easy to set up at a park.
