HARMONICS PLANNER

Harmonics work for you and against you. Your antenna can use them to cover many bands with one wire; your transmitter leaks them where they don't belong. This page handles both.

ANTENNA PLANNER

One Wire, Many Bands

A half-wave wire is also resonant at 2×, 3×, 4×… its frequency. Cut an end-fed for 3.550 MHz and the resonances fall at 7.100, 14.200, 21.300 and 28.400 — inside 40, 20, 15 and 10 metres. Pick the right starting frequency and you rarely need the tuner. Drag the slider to see each band move, or let the planner find the best spot.

A random wire with a 9:1 unun and a tuner works best when it is not a half-wave multiple on any band you use. At those lengths the feed point is a very high impedance and the tuner struggles. This finds lengths that stay clear on every band you pick.

THE BASICS

What Is A Harmonic?

No transmitter is perfectly pure. Alongside the signal you meant to send (the fundamental), it leaks small copies at whole-number multiples of that frequency. Transmit on 7.100 MHz and there is a faint signal at 14.200, 21.300, 28.400 MHz and up.

Why They Happen

Amplifiers are not perfectly linear. Any distortion of the wave shape creates energy at 2×, 3×, 4× the frequency. Pushing a final amplifier too hard makes it worse.

Many amplifiers cancel even harmonics, so the 3rd is often the strongest one left.

Why Portable Ops Should Care

A multiband end-fed or a random wire is resonant on many bands because those bands are harmonically related. That means your antenna radiates your 2nd and 3rd harmonics very efficiently.

At a campout, the next tent's receiver is a few feet away.

What The Rules Say

Under FCC Part 97.307, spurious emissions below 30 MHz must be at least 43 dB under your signal (for equipment in service after 2003); above 30 MHz, at least 60 dB. And if a harmonic causes harmful interference, you must fix it — meeting the number is not a free pass.

KEEPING IT CLEAN

Fixes That Work In The Field

A multiband wire radiates your harmonics too.

The same trick that makes one wire work on 40, 20, 15 and 10 means it happily radiates your transmitter's 2nd, 4th and 6th harmonics. That's the strongest argument for a low-pass filter on a portable end-fed.

Put a low-pass filter on the rig.

A low-pass filter goes between the radio (or amplifier) and the antenna. Its cutoff must be above your operating frequency but below the 2nd harmonic. The calculator tells you that window for any band. Multiband QRP filters exist that switch with the band.

Don't overdrive.

Too much mic gain, too much ALC, or a small amp pushed to full output all distort the signal. Back off until the ALC barely moves.

A tuner is not a filter.

Some tuner designs knock harmonics down a little; many do almost nothing. Don't count on one to clean up a dirty signal.

Use band-pass filters at multi-station events.

When two stations share a site, a band-pass filter on each receiver as well as clean transmitters is what makes them coexist. Field Day groups have done this for decades.

Listen for yourself.

Have a friend (or a WebSDR) tune to 2× and 3× your frequency while you transmit. If you can hear yourself loud on the harmonic nearby, so can the next tent.

Check it on the bench.

An inexpensive spectrum analyzer shows harmonics directly. Always go through a dummy load and enough attenuation — never connect a transmitter straight into the analyzer.
