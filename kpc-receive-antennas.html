RECEIVE-ONLY ANTENNAS

Hear what the noise is hiding. The Loop on Ground (LoG), Beverage on Ground, K9AY loops and active antennas: what they are, how to build them, and how to hook them up without frying your radio.

WHY BOTHER?WHICH ONE?BUILD A LoGBEVERAGE ON GROUNDTRANSFORMER HELPERHOOKING IT UPTESTING
THE BIG IDEA

Why a Receive Antenna?

On 160, 80 and 40 meters you can usually hear more noise than signal. A receive antenna doesn't make signals louder. It makes the noise quieter, so the signal stands out.

SIGNAL-TO-NOISE

Quieter Beats Louder

Your transmit antenna is built to radiate well, so it also picks up every noisy power line, charger and TV in the neighborhood. A small, low, "inefficient" receive antenna hears much less of that local noise. Signals read lower on the S-meter, but you copy them better.

LOW BANDS

Best on 160–40 m

Low-band noise is usually stronger than your radio's own internal noise, so you can throw away antenna gain and not lose anything. On 20 m and up, band noise is lower and these antennas help much less.

PORTABLE TOO

Great at the Campout

A LoG is 60 feet of wire on the grass and a little transformer. It packs in a sandwich bag. Lay one out away from the generators and campers and you'll hear 80 m stations nobody else can copy.

PICK ONE

Which Receive Antenna?

Start with the LoG. It's the cheapest, smallest and most forgiving.

EASIEST START HERE

Loop on Ground (LoG)

A square of insulated wire, about 15 ft per side, lying on the grass. Fed at one corner through a small transformer.

SPACE~20 × 20 ft
BANDS160–40 m (some use to 20 m)
PATTERNRoughly all-around
COSTUnder $20
DIRECTIONAL

Beverage on Ground (BOG)

A long, straight wire lying on the ground, aimed at the area you want to hear. A resistor to ground at the far end makes it one-way.

SPACE150–300 ft straight run
BANDS160–40 m
PATTERNToward the far end
COSTUnder $25
SWITCHABLE

K9AY Loops

Two triangle-shaped loops at right angles on one ~25 ft support, each with a resistor in it. A relay box picks one of four directions.

SPACE~30 × 30 ft
BANDS160 & 80 m (40 m OK)
PATTERN4 switchable directions
BEST FORHome station
TINY

Active Antennas

A small amplified whip ("mini-whip") or an amplified receive loop. Needs power over the coax. Popular for SDRs and web SDRs.

SPACEA pole or fence post
BANDSLF to 30 MHz
WATCH OUTWhips hear house noise; mount them away from the house
COST$30–300
STEP BY STEP

Build a Loop on Ground

About an hour of work. Parts: 60 ft of insulated wire, a small ferrite core, some magnet wire, a weatherproof box, a connector and a run of coax.

HOW IT LAYS OUT

Top View

The corner where the wire ends meet is the feedpoint. Bring both ends into the box and keep the coax leaving the loop at a right angle, along the ground.

THE PARTS

Shopping List

LOOP WIRE60 ft of insulated wire, 14–20 AWG. Anything works.
TRANSFORMERAbout 3:1 turns (9:1 impedance) with separate windings. A mix 73 or 43 binocular core (BN-73-202 or similar) is easy: 3 turns on the coax side, 9 on the loop side. Wiring diagram · Turns helper ↓
FEEDLINERG-6 TV coax (75 Ω) is cheap and perfect. F-connectors are fine.
CHOKEA common-mode choke at the radio end, and ideally one at the loop end. Choke recipes
PREAMPUsually needed: 15–25 dB of gain. Your radio's built-in preamp is a fine start.
PROTECTIONAn RX antenna jack, or a T/R switch that grounds the receive antenna when you transmit. See below
BUILD IT

Steps

  1. Wind the transformer. Pass magnet wire through both holes of the binocular core: 3 turns for the coax side, 9 turns for the loop side. Keep the two windings separate. They must not touch each other electrically. Check with an ohmmeter: no continuity between the two windings.
  2. Box it. Mount the core in a small plastic box with a coax connector (F or BNC) on one side and two screw terminals for the loop on the other.
  3. Lay out the loop. Make a square, about 15 ft on each side (60 ft total), right on the grass or a few inches above it. Lawn staples hold it down. Neat corners aren't important. A circle works the same.
  4. Connect it. Loop ends to the 9-turn winding, coax to the 3-turn winding. Run the coax away along the ground.
  5. Choke the coax near the radio (and at the loop if you can). Without it, the shield of the coax becomes the antenna and you lose the quiet.
  6. Into the radio's RX antenna input, through a preamp if signals are weak. Then do the short test.
DIRECTIONAL

Beverage on Ground (BOG)

A long wire on the ground that hears best in one direction. Point it at Europe, the West Coast, or wherever you want to work.

LAYOUT

Side View

It hears best toward the resistor end. Leave the resistor off and it hears both ways.

BUILD NOTES

What You Need

  • Wire: insulated, 150–300 ft, laid straight on the ground. Longer is more directional, but past ~300 ft it stops helping on 160/80.
  • Feed: a transformer with separate windings, 9:1 to start (same as the LoG). On very lossy ground a 4:1 sometimes matches better. Try both if you can.
  • Far end: a 390–560 Ω resistor (470 Ω is a good start) to a short ground rod. Tweak it while listening to a station off the back for the deepest null.
  • Near end: a ground rod for the transformer's coax side.
  • Mowers: it's a trip hazard and mower bait. Flag it, or tuck it along a fence line.
CALCULATOR

Receive Transformer Helper

Picks the turns for the little transformer between your receive antenna and the coax.

DON'T FRY IT

Hooking It Up

Your transmit antenna puts strong RF into anything nearby, including your receive antenna. Plan for that before you key up.

BEST

RX Antenna Jack

Many mid-range and higher radios have an "RX ANT" input or an RX-in/RX-out loop on the back. Use it. The radio picks the receive antenna on receive and takes care of switching when you transmit. Check your manual for the jack and the menu setting.

NO RX JACK?

T/R Switch

A receive-antenna switch box sits between the radio and both antennas. It listens on the receive antenna and shorts it to ground the moment you transmit. Use one that's keyed by your radio's amp-key / send line, not by RF sensing alone.

EITHER WAY

Distance & Protection

  • Keep the receive antenna as far as you can from the transmit antenna: 50 ft or more, more on 160 m.
  • Add a receiver front-end protector (limiter) in the line for extra insurance.
  • Don't run the RX coax right alongside the TX coax.
  • Run QRP if you're unsure. A preamp is the first thing to die.
IS IT WORKING?

Testing

  • The short test. Tune to a weak signal. Short the two loop terminals together (or pull the loop wire off). The signal should drop by more than 10 dB. If it barely changes, your coax shield is doing the receiving. Add or improve the choke.
  • Listen to the noise, not the S-meter. Switch between the TX antenna and the receive antenna. The receive antenna will read lower, but if the noise drops more than the signal, it's winning.
  • Turn the preamp on and off. If the band noise rises when the preamp is on, you have enough gain. If nothing changes, you need more.
  • Check for overload. Strong AM broadcast stations can swamp a preamp. If you hear AM radio mixed into 160 m, add a broadcast-band filter ahead of the preamp.
  • Move it. Receive antennas are fussy about location. Ten yards farther from the house or the power line can make a bigger difference than any tweak.
  • Compare with someone. At the campout, run your LoG next to someone's EFHW and swap headphones. You'll hear the difference fast.
