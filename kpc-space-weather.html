Plan An Activation

Pick a time in the next few days. Ratings use NOAA's predicted Kp for that 3-hour period, whether the Sun will be up at your location, and today's solar flux (which usually moves slowly).

Portable tip: don't stop at one band.

If 20m is quiet, check 17m, 15m, 30m or 40m. Space weather changes the odds; it does not replace listening.

Portable tip: work the greyline.

The half hour around your sunrise and sunset (shown at the top) is often the best DX window on 40m, 80m and 160m.

How To Read Kp

Kp 0–2 • QuietThe calm baseline for HF. Band openings still depend on solar flux, time of day and your station.
Kp 3–4 • Unsettled / ActivePaths get more variable, with more fading (QSB). Try more than one band before deciding it is dead.
Kp 5–9 • Storm (G1–G5)Expect weak, fluttery signals, especially on northern and polar paths. Regional 40m contacts often still work.

HF OPERATOR VIEW

What Do These Numbers Mean For Radio?

No single number tells you whether a band is open. Combine solar activity, geomagnetic activity, time of day, band, antenna, noise level and what you actually hear.

Solar Flux & Sunspots

SFI and sunspot number track how strongly the Sun is ionizing the F layer. Roughly: under 90 favours 40m–20m; 100–140 brings 17m and 15m to life; above 140 is when 12m and 10m shine.

Kp & A-index

Kp is the 3-hour geomagnetic reading; A is the daily-scale version. Low is good. Kp 5+ is a storm: expect fading, flutter and weak northern paths. High flux with low Kp is the sweet spot.

Bz, Solar Wind & X-Rays

Sustained southward (negative) Bz with fast wind (500+ km/s) usually means Kp is about to rise. X-ray class shows flares: C is minor, M5+ can black out the sunlit side, X is major.

KPC FIELD CHECK

What Does This Mean For My Activation?

Green-light setup

Quiet Kp, no R/S/G warning, decent solar flux, and bands producing signals when you listen. Set up, call CQ and see what happens.

Be flexible

Elevated Kp or active solar wind does not mean “stay home.” Try different bands and directions, and lean on 40m and 30m for regional hunters.

Flare blackout (R-scale)

If the bands suddenly go silent in daylight, check the X-ray and D-RAP above. Blackouts usually ease within an hour; higher bands recover first.

Storm conditions (G-scale)

Expect variability for a day or more. CW and FT8 get through when SSB cannot, and regional paths often survive when DX is gone.

PLAIN-ENGLISH GLOSSARY

New To This? Start Here.

SFI (Solar Flux)
How much the Sun is energizing the ionosphere. Higher means the upper bands (15m, 12m, 10m) are more likely to open.
Sunspot Number
A count of active regions on the Sun. It rises and falls with SFI over the 11-year solar cycle.
Kp
How disturbed Earth's magnetic field is, 0 to 9, updated every 3 hours. Low is good for HF; 5 or more is a storm.
A-index
The same disturbance as Kp, averaged over a day. Under 10 is quiet; over 30 is stormy.
Bz
The north–south tilt of the magnetic field carried by the solar wind. Negative (southward) for a while means Kp is likely to climb.
X-ray class
Flare strength: A, B, C, M, X, each ten times stronger. Big M and X flares cause daylight radio blackouts.
R / S / G scales
NOAA's 1–5 ratings for radio blackouts, solar radiation storms and geomagnetic storms.
Greyline
The band of twilight around sunrise and sunset. Low-band signals often travel unusually far along it.
NVIS
Near Vertical Incidence Skywave: sending the signal almost straight up so it lands within a few hundred miles. A low wire on 40m or 80m does it well — ideal for in-state contacts.
MUF
Maximum Usable Frequency: the highest frequency the ionosphere will bend back to Earth on a given path right now.
QSB
Fading. Signals that rise and fall, common when Kp is elevated.
Sporadic-E
Short-lived patches of ionization that open 10m and 6m with little warning, mostly late spring and summer.
