KPC FIELD INTERPRETATION

What Does This Mean For My Activation?

Space weather is only one part of propagation, but it can explain why a band suddenly feels great, flat, noisy, or unstable.

📻 HF Conditions

Quiet geomagnetic conditions are generally easier for HF operations. Higher geomagnetic activity can make propagation more variable, while solar activity can support higher-frequency bands when other conditions cooperate.

☀️ Solar Activity

Higher solar activity can increase ionization in the ionosphere and can help higher HF bands such as 15, 12 and 10 meters. It does not guarantee a good opening — time of day, frequency, location and geomagnetic conditions still matter.

🌎 Geomagnetic Activity

Geomagnetic storms can disturb the ionosphere and make HF paths unstable. A quiet or low K index is generally a more comfortable starting point for portable HF planning.

UNDERSTANDING THE NUMBERS

K Index, Solar Flux & Sunspots

🧲 K Index

The planetary K index describes short-term disturbances in Earth’s magnetic field on a 0–9 scale. 0–2 is generally quiet, 3 is unsettled, 4 is active, and 5 or higher indicates storm conditions. For portable HF work, a lower K index is generally a better starting point for stable paths.

☀️ Solar Flux (F10.7)

The 10.7 cm solar flux is a standard measure of solar radio emission and is widely used as an indicator of solar activity. Higher flux can mean stronger ionization of the ionosphere and better support for higher HF bands, especially when geomagnetic conditions are quiet.

🔭 Sunspots

Sunspot number tracks active regions visible on the Sun. Sunspots are associated with stronger solar activity, but the number by itself does not tell you whether your band will be open. Combine it with solar flux, K index, time of day, season, and what you actually hear on the band.

FIELD CHECK

How To Read The Conditions

Low K + healthy solar activity

Often a good combination to try 15m, 17m and 20m, then move higher or lower depending on what you hear.

R alerts

Radio-blackout alerts are tied to solar flares. A strong flare can reduce or black out HF propagation on the sunlit side of Earth.

High K / G-level activity

Geomagnetic disturbances can make HF paths noisy, unstable, or weaker than expected. Try different bands and be prepared to change plans.

Sunspots + flux

These indicate solar activity, not a promise of propagation. Your antenna, power, location, time, season and the other station still matter.

HF BAND GUIDE

Which Bands Should I Try?

These are practical starting points, not guarantees. Listen, tune, and test more than one band when you arrive at the park.

20m

Often a dependable all-around portable HF band with good daytime reach.

17m

A useful middle ground when propagation supports it, often quieter than lower bands.

15m

Can become very productive when solar activity and ionization cooperate.

12m

More dependent on favorable conditions, but can surprise you with strong openings.

10m

Highly variable; excellent when open and much quieter when conditions are poor.

NOAA SCALE EXPLAINED

The Three Space-Weather Scales

NOAA uses three 1–5 scales. They describe different hazards, so an R2, S2 and G2 are not the same thing.

R — Radio Blackout

Driven by solar flares. R1 is minor; R5 is extreme. The primary concern for KPC operators is HF radio degradation or blackout on the sunlit side of Earth.

KPC QUICK GUIDE

Before You Set Up The Station

Start with the K index.

If it is low and conditions are quiet, your HF planning has a calmer starting point.

Look at solar flux.

Higher values can support higher-frequency HF propagation, especially when the ionosphere is otherwise favorable.

Check R, S and G alerts.

A current alert does not automatically mean your activation will fail, but it tells you why conditions may be unusual.

Then listen.

Space weather is one part of propagation. Band, time, antenna, power, noise level, location and the stations on the other end still matter.
