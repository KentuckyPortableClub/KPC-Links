KIDS ROOM • GUIDE 1

HOW RADIO WAVES TRAVEL

How can a little radio in Kentucky talk to someone in Japan with no phone, no internet and no wires? Let's find out!

← KIDS ROOMGUIDE 2: WHY HAM RADIO MATTERS →

1What is a radio wave?

A radio wave is invisible energy that zooms through the air. Think of dropping a pebble in a pond: ripples spread out in every direction. A radio antenna makes ripples too, but you can't see them, and they don't need water. They can even travel through space!

🤯 Super fast!

Radio waves travel at the speed of light: about 186,000 miles every second. That's fast enough to go around the whole Earth 7 times in one second.

Every radio wave has a frequency: how many waves pass by each second. Hams also talk about wavelength: how long one wave is from bump to bump. That's why ham bands have names like "the 20-meter band" (each wave is about 20 meters long, as long as a school bus and a half) or "2 meters" (about as tall as a grown-up).

2Three ways radio waves get there

🔦Line of sightShort waves (like the 2-meter band and walkie-talkies) go in a straight line, like a flashlight beam. Hills and buildings block them, so hams put antennas up high. A repeater on a tall tower or mountaintop catches your signal and sends it back out, much farther.
🌊Ground waveLonger waves can hug the curve of the Earth for a while, like water flowing over rocks. AM radio stations use ground wave during the day.
🏓Skywave ("skip")Shortwave bands (like 40, 20 and 10 meters) can shoot up to the ionosphere and bounce back down hundreds or thousands of miles away. Sometimes they bounce again off the ground and go up for another hop, like skipping a stone across a lake. That's how a small radio can reach around the world!

🤔 The skip zone mystery

Sometimes a station 1,000 miles away hears you perfectly, but your friend 200 miles away can't hear you at all. That's because your bounce came down past them, and they were too far for the ground wave. Hams call that the skip zone.

3The sun runs the show

The ionosphere is made by sunlight. The sun's energy charges up the thin air high above us, and that charged air is what bounces radio waves. So the sun changes how far radio can go:

  • ☀️ Daytime: higher bands like 20, 15 and 10 meters are often open to faraway places.
  • 🌙 Nighttime: lower bands like 40 and 80 meters wake up and travel farther. (Try it: an AM radio at night can pick up stations hundreds of miles away!)
  • 🌞 Sunspots: about every 11 years the sun gets extra busy with sunspots. More sunspots = a stronger ionosphere = better bands for long-distance contacts.
  • ⚡ Solar flares: a big burst from the sun can make the bands go quiet for a while. Hams call that a radio blackout.

4Amazing radio tricks

🌕Moon bounceSome hams bounce signals off the Moon and hear them come back to Earth. That's about a 477,000-mile round trip!
☄️Meteor scatterShooting stars leave a quick trail of charged air. Hams can bounce signals off it for just a second or two.
🛰️SatellitesHams have their own satellites! With a handheld radio and a hand-held antenna, you can talk through a satellite as it zooms overhead.
🚀Space stationAstronauts on the International Space Station have ham radios. Kids at schools have talked to them!
🌅Gray lineThe line between day and night (sunrise and sunset) circles the Earth. Signals traveling along it can go extra far.

5Try it yourself

🔬 Radio experiments

  • AM radio at night: scan an AM radio after dark and write down the farthest city you hear. Try again in the daytime. What changed?
  • Hand it a hill: with walkie-talkies and a grown-up, test how far you can talk on flat ground, then with a hill or a building in between.
  • Listen online: ask a grown-up to help you open a WebSDR (a radio you can tune in your web browser) and listen to hams around the world.
  • Check the bands: the KPC Radio Conditions page shows which bands are open right now and where the gray line is.

📖Words to know

FrequencyHow many waves go by each second.
WavelengthHow long one wave is.
BandA group of frequencies, like "the 20-meter band."
IonosphereCharged air high above Earth that bounces radio waves.
SkipWhen a signal bounces off the ionosphere to a faraway place.
RepeaterA radio on a tall tower that re-sends your signal farther.
PropagationThe fancy word for how radio waves travel.
AntennaThe part that sends and catches radio waves.
